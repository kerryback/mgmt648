# Mini-Case 2A: The Sunk Cost Trap at Meridian Pharma

## Background

Meridian Pharmaceuticals spent $15 million over three years developing a new topical pain relief cream. The compound works but performed only modestly better than over-the-counter competitors in clinical trials. The marketing team now proposes launching the cream nationally, projecting $8 million in annual revenue and $6 million in annual operating costs (excluding any allocation of the $15 million already spent). The launch would require $4 million in new manufacturing equipment (5-year straight-line depreciation, zero salvage value) and $1.2 million in initial working capital. Meridian's tax rate is 30%, and its cost of capital for projects of this risk is 12%.

Before the launch decision is made, a specialty pharmacy chain offers to license the compound from Meridian for a one-time payment of $5 million.

## Questions

1. Meridian's CFO insists the $15 million R&D cost must be "recovered" before the project can be approved. Is this correct? Why or why not?

2. Calculate the free cash flow for Year 1 of the launch project.

3. What is the relevant cost that the $15 million R&D creates in the launch-vs.-license decision, if any? How does the $5 million license offer change the analysis?

4. Should Meridian launch the product or take the license fee? What additional information would sharpen the recommendation?

## Key Concepts

- Sunk costs are irrelevant to forward-looking investment decisions
- Only incremental cash flows matter
- Opportunity cost: by choosing to launch, Meridian foregoes the $5 million license fee — that foregone amount is a real cost of launching
- The relevant initial outlay for the launch decision is $4M equipment + $1.2M working capital + $5M opportunity cost = $10.2M
