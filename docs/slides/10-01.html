Valuing the whole firm

Two claims, one stream

The operations produce one stream of cash. Debt and equity divide it.

Debt

Interest and principal, fixed by contract, paid before anything reaches the shareholders.

Equity

Whatever is left, with no ceiling and no promise.

Value the stream once and then divide it. Forecasting the equity’s share directly means forecasting the borrowing and repayment schedule as well.

Leave interest out of the cash flows

Interest leaves the shareholders and arrives at the lenders. Both are inside the group being valued, so the payment nets to zero and never appears.

Operating cash flow less investment

No line for interest, and the tax rate is applied to EBIT rather than to EBIT less interest. The deduction for interest is carried in the discount rate.

The rate has to match the claim

The cash flow belongs to both groups, so the rate is the WACC: the blend of what each group requires, weighted by the market value of what each holds. We calculate it on October 10 and take it as given today.

What the present value buys

Two stages

Growth above the economy ends

A company growing faster than the economy forever eventually exceeds it. So the forecast is built in two parts, and the second part is the one that has to be sustainable.

  1. Years 1 to \(n\), forecast each year on its own: sales, margin, the ratios
  2. Year \(n+1\) onward, one growth rate that never changes

The ceiling on the second stage

2.5% real GDP growth a year

2.2% the GDP price deflator

4.8% nominal GDP growth

Thirty years through 2025. Real growth and prices compound rather than add: \(1.025 \times 1.022 = 1.048\). Over the full run since 1947 nominal growth was 6.4% a year, of which 3.1 points were real and 3.2 were prices.

Bureau of Economic Analysis, gross domestic product in current and in chained 2017 dollars, annual. FRED series GDPA and GDPCA. A second-stage rate above this is a forecast that the company eventually becomes the whole economy.

The perpetuity formula

\[\text{TV}_n = \frac{\text{FCF}_{n+1}}{r - g}\]

The numerator is the year after the last forecast year. The formula assumes \(g\) is constant forever, that the ratios are constant too, and that \(g\) stays below \(r\), the WACC.

The terminal value is dated \(n\)

\(\text{TV}_n\) is a value at the end of year \(n\), standing in for every cash flow from year \(n+1\) on. It gets discounted \(n\) periods, alongside \(\text{FCF}_n\).

\[\text{EV} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{\text{FCF}_t}{(1+r)^t} \;+\; \frac{\text{TV}_n}{(1+r)^n}\]

Discounting the terminal value \(n+1\) periods divides it by an extra \(1+k\). Check the exponent against the exponent on \(\text{FCF}_n\): they are the same.

From enterprise value to a price

  1. Subtract debt, at book value unless it trades far from it
  2. Add cash and anything else the operations do not use
  3. Divide by diluted shares

Each balance sheet item belongs in exactly one place: inside the forecast, if it produces the cash flows being discounted, or in this calculation, if it does not. Counting it twice or leaving it out of both are the two failures.

Ways to calculate free cash flow

\[\begin{aligned} \text{FCF} &= \text{NOPAT} + \text{Depreciation} - \text{CapEx} - \Delta \text{NWC}\\[2pt] &= \text{NOPAT} - \Delta \text{Net PP\&E} - \Delta \text{NWC}\\[2pt] &= \text{NOPAT} - \Delta \text{IC} \end{aligned}\]

The first line to the second

Net PP&E rolls forward as last year’s balance plus capital expenditure less depreciation. So capital expenditure less depreciation is the change in the balance, and two forecast lines collapse into one.

The second line to the third

Net PP&E and net working capital are the two pieces of invested capital, so their changes add to the change in invested capital.
